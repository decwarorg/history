
# Notes around Motivations, Septemeber 2026

# **History**

Now let's walk through my history with Decwar step by step, starting with 1984 and 1985\. I was a 15- or 16-year-old high schooler in Hays County, south of Austin, 15 to 20 minutes south of Austin and 15 to 20 minutes north of San Marcos. My high school was out in the country, and we were taking summer courses at Southwest Texas State University (SWT) in San Marcos. They had a computer science department with a fairly new computer installation: a DEC-10, which was considered the sibling of the DEC-10 in Austin. The Austin DEC-10 on which Decwar was created was the sibling of the DEC-10 down in San Marcos.

At SWT, I got to use the DEC-10 as a teenager in 1984, and I was immediately aware of Decwar because it was overwhelmingly the most popular thing to do. If you were in the computer building, you were well aware of Decwar because it was a raging success. No other game on the DEC-10 was even comparable; Decwar was overwhelmingly the rage. On a Friday afternoon with 18 people all in the same building playing, there would be very exciting, raging Decwar tournaments with the sounds of laughter and cries of anguish ringing out throughout the halls. This was extremely exciting for a teenager.

We were playing mostly in the main terminal room, just a large room with maybe 50 terminals, mostly VT52s and VT100s, and some old teletypes left over. Sometimes five or six of our high school friends would travel down together for a Decwar tournament. People in my high school 15 minutes north of San Marcos were well aware of Decwar, so we would all go together as a group. There were other groups of teenagers, and of course, many college students and faculty playing from their offices. There was a very strong local culture and scene around the game with these teenagers coming in, showing elements of the underground hacker culture and BBS users. It was a permissive environment where these odd characters were allowed in, leaving indelible memories and a deep mark on me. It encouraged me to set my course that computers were a very good space for me, feeling accepted and comfortable in a higher-level mainframe and academic computing environment.

By 1986 and 1987, I was preparing to graduate from Hays High and go to UT Austin, moving 15 to 20 minutes north. My focus changed to astronomy, but I was still interested in computers. By my junior year at UT, I got a research assistant position, my first user account on a bigger mainframe/minicomputer, and my first internet email address: `noah@astro.utexas.edu`. I was on the internet and a Usenet user then, getting internet mail and newsgroups in 1988 and 1989\. Immediately, I joined the Usenet groups about the DEC-10 and PDP-10 (`alt.sys.pdp10`) and searched for any news about Decwar because I still loved and remembered the game.

Around 1989 and 1990, I was on Usenet and aware of names we are now familiar with from Decwar, like Clive Dawson and Harris Newman (and probably Merlyn Cousins). I was already aware of Clives's *The Soul of a New Machine* about the UT DEC-10. Decwar was vanishing everywhere except on CompuServe (where it was Mega Wars), but I was not on CompuServe; I was just interested in the history of Decwar. This stayed true all through the 90s. I kept an eye on `alt.sys.pdp10`, following stories and looking for information. I was well aware of Harris Newman, who was loudly and persistently searching for Decwar throughout the 90s, a voice crying in the wilderness.

To close out the 80s and 90s, I did get in contact directly with some of these people via Usenet and email, especially Harris Newman. Around 2000, there were hints and evidence that the source code had appeared, which triggered me to contact Harris Newman. Like others have noted, the interaction was a bit strange, and the situation around the source code was left mysterious. Around 2000, DEC-10 and PDP-10 emulators were going strong, and I thought about beginning to experiment with hardware emulation, but because I didn't have hands-on access to the Decwar code, I postponed it. If I had gotten my hands on the source code around 2000, I certainly would have begun using PDP-10 hardware emulators and running it. 26 years ago\!

That was delayed, and the next thing we heard about the game was in 2011\. Anyone watching for news via Usenet or other PDP-10 venues was fully aware that the Briscoe Center for American history had cataloged items and that the source code appeared from nowhere at Briscoe in 2011\. I knew that, but I was finishing my PhD, and my daughter was born in Germany in June 2011 while I was still finishing my PhD in Austin, so things were quite stressful personally. I wasn't able to dig into it. If it had been 2000, I would have had the time and bandwidth; in 2011, there was no way.

We now know Harris Newman had the source code from 1995 and sat on it for 16 years before it became available at Briscoe. Merlyn Cousins immediately re-entered the story and did legendary work, using the old 60s debugger DDT to remove CompuServe-specific specializations so the source code could run on standard TOPS-10. Merlyn did that in 2011\.

From 2011 until 2024, 13 years, I was completely busy working in industry and raising my young daughter. The next important event was in 2024, when Oscar, Lars, and the Obsolescence Guaranteed team released the reconstruction of the PDP-10 front panel, and there was a big news article on the Guardian website. I saw it the same day and ordered it later that night. All through that period I had been busy, but when I saw that Guardian article in summer 2024, I immediately got the PDP-10 emulator software working on my laptop. I contacted Merlin and learned how to run the game using the source code he had made usable (deactivating CompuServe calls so it ran on standard TOPS-10). Within two weeks of seeing that Guardian article and ordering the kit, I had Decwar building and running on a PDP-10 emulator on my laptop.

I immediately began discussing this in the PDP-10 Google Group (the helpline for the Obsolescence Guaranteed front panel kit, which had roughly a thousand kits). I discussed how I got it running and even helped someone else get it running on their laptop.

In the second half of 2024, the front panel kit arrived, and I built it successfully, running Decwar with the reconstructed front panel. But I was already looking ahead. Decwar alone is a problem because nothing happens; you need automated robots to play. I knew we needed automated robots, at least 10 robots (5 on each side), so human players could join into a game with plenty of action. The other clear objective was to productionize and improve the workflow for building and running the game. I envisioned Docker containers and the same concept Lars uses with the "Time Capsule" making it so anyone can enter the past and everything just works.

At the time, things were primitive. I was using Kermit for file transfer because tape emulation on the hardware was tricky, but Kermit was slow and awkward. I really wanted to use tape, put it all in a Docker container, and make it simple. By early 2025, I got tape working and started working on the Docker container, which was working well by late 2025 so anyone could use it.

I did a tremendous amount of work getting that tooling and workflow going without having truly begun to reconstruct the original UT source distribution tape. The source code was still in the strange form Harris received by email in 1995, cataloged at Briscoe, and fixed by Merlin for TOPS-10, complete with CompuServe additions and scrambled together like a junk mountain with no natural structure because CompuServe was running a business for paying customers. As I worked over that year, I realized all the necessary clues were there, completely scrambled together like a scrambled egg. I needed to undo the egg scrambling to get back to the original egg.

Once my environment, tooling, workflows, and Docker containers were working well and I had robots playing, I stepped back to do digital history and archeology: carefully analyzing and reconstructing the original UT source distribution tape. By early 2026, I had a complete, easy-to-use package with three Docker containers:

1. One devoted to the DEC-10, TOPS-10, and the game itself, built from an accurate reconstruction of the source distribution tape.  
2. One for the automated Python-based robot players.  
3. A brand-new JavaScript/Node.js GUI front-end container that I developed after discussing with Eric Freeman to display the Galaxy—something that didn't exist in the 80s because graphics hardware was too specialized back then.

At that point, we had a robust reconstruction of the original game for recreational use. The focus turned toward interfacing with people, and Eric, MK, and I created `decwar.org` and began exploring how to do the right thing. This year, I've been working heavily on websites, social media, and an entire dissertation loosely around these topics. Eric and I started drafting a book focused on the game around the beginning of 2026 and have returned to that, we need to get a focused book out.

My separate project will keep evolving, but I'll leave this recording here and do a separate recording about why I'm qualified to be involved in this, which should be clear by now.

# San Marcos Texas 1984

Apr 13, 2024

in 83 or 84 came something special - ‘getting to know’ the pdp-10 / dec10 at swt in san marcos texas. southwest texas state university. this was a dec10 that had been ‘handed down’ by swt’s ‘big brother’ in austin. coming straight from the utaustin ‘source’, it naturally had decwar installed - that calls for a dedicated discussion. ‘adventure’ was there as well, and other classics.

curiously enough, it was housed in a 70s building very similar to rlm hall at utaustin. it wouldn’t be a surprise if they were built by the same firm. the building was on the side of a hill and a small foot bridge connected the street to a glass entryway, then a hallway to the right led past the terminal room to the glassed-off computer room.

in the terminal room, the everyday environment on vt-52 or vt-100 was tops-10 sos text editor. this was the ‘best practice’ command line utility for undergrads learning ‘programming’. sos seems to have been the easy / intro alternative to teco. teco definitely had the reputation of being tough, with command lines compared to ‘noise’. sos was the choice for newbies at swt - laminated ‘sos commands’ cards were for sale at the university bookstore.

compilers were fortran, lisp, pascal, cobol. it seems ‘c’ was more of a distant rumble in that particular environment - unix certainly was - not clear how much awareness of unix there even was. probably comparable with vms awareness.

decwriter printer terminals were still common, and if all the crt terminals were occupied it was normal to use one. this was essentially an overgrown typewriter, with fanfold computer paper fed up and through from a cardboard box sitting on the floor beneath. line feed and carriage return had real meaning - it was a typewriter.

there were systems using punchcards, and it wasn’t uncommon to run a batch job via a punchcard reader. after a job was submitted, output was a thick stack of fanfold paper placed in an output box by operators in the computer room.

having a user account on the dec10, and being able to logon to one of the terminals, was the important thing. ‘online chat’ was possible with other users. usually they were sitting hidden somewhere in the terminal room. and there was a form of email with other users. it was a classic time sharing multi-user system, exactly as described in the stephen levy book.

memory is that typical ‘all out’ decwar games, san marcos 83/84, were around thirty to forty minutes, and an ideal afternoon would be two or three intense games complete with ‘report to slave planet’ or ‘the galaxy is saved’ messages. then go swimming/tubing in the san marcos river. as project utexas and robots have gotten smoother over the last few months, this has led to conscious ‘tuning’ of the game length to be thirty to forty minutes with ten robots playing. with decwarjs now coming online, this topic is getting deeper. what are the ‘knobs’ controlling game length?

right now the robots are in defense mode when young and attack enemy ships, then switch to offense when older and attack enemy bases. victory condition is simply ‘enemy has zero bases’ but this is not right. historical victory condition is ‘zero planets and enemy has no bases’. the control knob is then the initial number of planets. the default is sixty, but there’s strong evidence it was varied between twenty and eighty to control game length. twenty planets would mean a short game.

what’s needed in project utexas is for the robots to be a bit smarter. they should take out planets by either building them into bases or destroying them. probably this behaviour should be active on both defense and offense. game length could then be tuned via the initial number of planets.

memory is that fifteen year old beginners basically only cared about planets when number of bases got low. then there was a rush to capture and build planets to get number of bases back up. with fewer initial planets, they would ‘run out’ earlier and prevent building bases. then the end would come quickly. but there’s also a much dimmer memory that there were times when a general ‘planet hunt’ would break out, and planets were destroyed as quickly as possible. this would make sense if the enemies base count was low. get all the planets and the enemies last base. and there’s even a very dim memory that the smarter and more strategic players would lead these ‘planet hunts’. something like a rallying call over the radio to ‘take out the planets’. these memories are very slowly returning now. could be dreaming them, but do suspect they’re real.
