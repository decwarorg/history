## Discussions in late 2025 with Joel and Noah

In an informal discussion, we came to the view that a session lasting roughly thirty to forty minutes is often about right. On a good day, with a solid group of players, a two to three hour afternoon can comfortably include two or three full games. That pace seems especially well suited to a casual, satisfying experience: enough time for real strategic play to matter, but not so long that the session becomes exhausting or overly demanding. For many players, two or three hours is a natural and rewarding window, whereas anything longer can start to feel burdensome.

The exact duration depends heavily on the number of players, their skill level, and the design of the game itself, including factors such as the number of planets and the complexity of the strategic environment. Still, the basic principle is clear: a casual player can easily enjoy two or three complete games in an afternoon without needing any extraordinary effort or commitment. That creates the right rhythm for a game that is strategic in substance but still accessible and thrilling. A well-designed game should allow the deeper tactical and coordination elements to emerge within a limited time, rather than stretching the experience beyond the point where it remains relaxed and engaging.

A strong indicator of a good game is that the strategic layer has time to matter: border regions form, damaged ships are towed back from enemy territory, and coordinated action becomes essential. The radio chatter should reflect teamwork rather than simple noise or posturing, with players making requests, sharing directions, and responding to one another as part of a larger plan. Thirty to forty minutes is enough for these dynamics to develop and for strategy to influence outcomes, but it is not so long that casual players are discouraged. More serious players may prefer a longer session, perhaps closer to an hour, but the broader point is that the game should feel complete without becoming prolonged.

The historical context of the CompuServe era helps explain how game length evolved in practice. Sessions grew longer because players were paying by the minute and because the host environment needed to sustain many simultaneous players across a shared machine. Developers had to balance the demands of a large multi-user system with the need to keep players engaged. In that environment, game length was shaped not only by player preference, but by the practical realities of infrastructure, economics, and multiplayer design. The incentives and tradeoffs were different.
